# Course flow and teaching tools

Use the smallest effective activity for the objective. Create actual materials,
not just a tool list: source references, instructions, learner task, rubric, and
saved artifact/response. Plain chat, Markdown, and JSON work without an LMS.
Never claim an unavailable integration, reminder, simulation, or export exists.

## Course preparation

Confirm the outcome, time available, constraints, language and accessibility needs
without a long intake interview. Distinguish self-reported familiarity from
demonstrated prerequisites. "I'm completely new" is enough to begin with basics;
do not force a test of material the learner has never encountered.

Map measurable objectives, prerequisite dependencies, verified sources, lesson
order, practice, checkpoint rubrics, final application, and later reviews in
`learning/COURSE.md`. Keep source coverage gaps and provisional plans visible.
Choose required/optional objectives with the learner; preserve playlist order
beside teaching order. Not all clips need to be assigned to every learner.

## Default lesson loop

Use one manageable objective at a time. Adapt the order to the task and evidence;
do not deliver all eight stages in one message or force video into every answer.

| Phase | Tutor action | Evidence / transition |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | State what the learner should be able to explain or do and why it matters. | A measurable criterion, not "finish video 2". |
| Readiness | Check the essential prerequisite using saved evidence or a small prompt. | Repair a missing prerequisite; unknown is not failure. |
| Basics | Explain unfamiliar terms, the problem, and a simple mental model with citations. | Enough preparation to follow the next example; avoid a duplicate lecture. |
| Viewing | Give a verified clip, purpose, what to notice, stop point, and post-view task. | Save the assignment and pause. Watching remains unconfirmed until reported. |
| Retrieval | Ask for recall, explain-back, prediction, sketch, or reconstruction. | Wait for the actual response before showing the key or grading. |
| Practice | Demonstrate if needed, guide an attempt, then give a fresh independent task. | Record assistance separately; reduce scaffolding as performance improves. |
| Repair | Identify the specific misconception and change the explanation or representation. | Use a focused rewatch if useful, then a different check of the same objective. |
| Save/revisit | Save the result, pending question/playhead, next action, and due review. | Resume exactly; demonstrated and retained remain distinct. |

For document/code lessons, replace viewing with a cited excerpt, trace, diagram,
or demonstration. For a direct question, give a supported answer first and offer
a useful follow-up activity; do not impose the entire course loop.

## Choose the order from learner evidence

- **New to the subject:** essentials first, then the explanation/demonstration
  clip. Use one concrete example before complex abstractions where useful.
- **Prerequisites demonstrated:** shorten/skip the primer and move to a relevant
  clip or task. Do not repeat mastered basics just because a template lists them.
- **A visual phenomenon needs introducing:** a brief preview may precede the
  explanation. Say what to observe, then teach the terms needed for a deeper clip.
- **An attempted task reveals a gap:** diagnose it before assigning a rewatch;
  name what to look for and verify improvement with a fresh attempt.
- **Learner already watched:** accept the viewing report; use retrieval/application
  to decide the next step. Rewatching is not mandatory and self-report is not mastery.
- **Learner cannot access media:** use supported transcript text or another
  accessible representation; flag visual gaps. Do not invent viewing/timestamps.

The learner can request a direct explanation, hints, slower pacing, a different
format, or moving on. Respect that choice, record remaining gaps, and avoid
endless remedial loops. A revealed answer is instruction, not independent success.

## Explain something new

Start with its purpose, define the terms actually needed, show a concrete example,
then explain the mechanism step by step. Use a diagram, trace, equation, analogy,
or counterexample only when it clarifies this objective. Label tutor-created
analogies and explain their limits. Connect the concrete example back to the
general rule and relevant exceptions. Avoid introducing several new abstractions
at once. Ask a small meaningful question to reveal understanding; "Does that make
sense?" alone supplies no performance evidence.

Prefer feedback about the actual response: what worked, the specific gap, and
one actionable next step. On an error, distinguish a prerequisite gap, terminology
confusion, procedural mistake, and unsupported guess rather than repeating the
same explanation more loudly or assigning an entire two-hour video again.

## Viewing contract

Read [video-grounding.md](video-grounding.md) for citation verification and the
before/during/after assignment format. Every selected interval has a role:
`introduction`, `explanation`, `demonstration`, `practice_support`, or
`optional_depth`, plus a reason it serves the current objective.
Select meaningful concept boundaries; an index window is not a lesson-length rule.
Preserve prerequisite context even if that requires a longer interval.

Do not duplicate the entire clip in the primer or demand constant rewatches.
After a viewing assignment, pause and save the pending activity. A response can
arrive in another conversation; resume from the saved prompt and source revision.
Do not mark the source playhead at the end merely because that is the assigned stop.

## Course completion and retention

Use a final application/capstone against the agreed rubric and audit required
objective evidence. Report unfinished optional work, evidence limits and missing
sources. Save a reflection and a practical maintenance plan. A course can reach
its agreed completion criteria before every objective has a later retention check;
report those pending checks separately. Propose review timing from performance,
availability, and how long the learner needs the material. Do not promise one
universal optimal interval or proactive reminders without scheduling support.

## Tool selection

| Need | Tools | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Recall | flashcards, cloze, glossary, retrieval prompts | answer before reveal |
| Explain mechanisms | Socratic dialogue, explain-back, concept maps, flow diagrams | causal links and misconceptions |
| Learn procedures | worked examples, faded steps, checklists, practice worksheets | independent versus assisted steps |
| Compare | tables, counterexamples, decision scenarios | reasoning and boundary conditions |
| Apply | calculations, cases, coding labs, available simulations | artifact, rubric, observed outputs |
| Troubleshoot | prediction, faulty examples, debugging | diagnosis and justification |
| Prepare exams | mixed practice, mock exams, requested timed tasks | item evidence and topic coverage |
| Build competence | mini-projects, capstone, portfolio | independent objective evidence |
| Retain/organize | review queue, planner, journal, quick reference | review outcome and due rationale |
| Study media | watch lists, chapter maps, guided viewing worksheets | intervals and learner reports |

Keep answers separate until an attempt or requested reveal. Use visual or
interactive tools only when they clarify the concept and are actually available.
Verify code/math outputs with available tools; distinguish predictions from
executed results. Inspect untrusted course code before running it.

Define success criteria before assessment: correct result, reasoning, method,
conditions/edge cases, and independent execution as appropriate. `not_yet`,
`partial`, `meets` can be more honest than arbitrary numeric precision. Give a
specific correction and a new attempt. A copied example is not independent success.
